Mad Honey
Hidden Risks

It is sold in jars, honey sticks, gummies and brightly colored packages. Traditional mad honey can contain a plant toxin that slows the heart and drops blood pressure.

See Real ProductsGet the Facts

It looks like honey. The effects can be anything but sweet.

Traditional “mad honey” contains grayanotoxins from certain rhododendron flowers. These toxins can disrupt the electrical activity of nerves and the heart. Poisoning has caused severe dizziness, vomiting, dangerously low blood pressure, slowed heart rate and fainting.

Meanwhile, the term “mad honey” appears on gummies, sticks, pouches and branded blends. These are not automatically chemically equivalent products. The label alone cannot tell parents what is inside.

Mad honey, explained in plain English

Parents should be able to recognize the products and understand the actual medical danger, without confusing mad honey with opioids or assuming every branded gummy contains the same ingredient.

What is mad honey?

Traditional mad honey is honey made by bees collecting nectar from certain rhododendron plants. It can contain grayanotoxins, naturally occurring poisons that affect voltage-gated sodium channels. This is different from ordinary table honey.

Is mad honey an opioid?

No. Grayanotoxins do not act like mitragynine, 7-OH, or tianeptine at opioid receptors. Mad honey belongs in a retail-intoxicant awareness guide because it can cause serious poisoning, especially cardiovascular toxicity. It should not be described as heroin or as an opioid.

What happens when someone eats it?

Poisoning may cause nausea, vomiting, sweating, dizziness, weakness, blurred vision, confusion, very slow heart rate, low blood pressure and fainting. Severe cases may cause dangerous abnormal heart rhythms. Effects can begin within minutes to a few hours.

Why are low heart rate and blood pressure so serious?

A marked fall in blood pressure or heart rate can reduce blood flow to the brain and other organs. A person may collapse, become confused or lose consciousness. Hospital treatment may require monitoring, intravenous fluids and medications such as atropine.

Are gummies and honey sticks the same as traditional mad honey?

Not necessarily. The phrase “mad honey” is used on products with different formats and marketing claims. Gummies and blends may contain other ingredients, and photographs cannot establish their composition. A laboratory report must identify the specific compounds and their concentrations.

Does a 25,000 mg label mean 25,000 mg of grayanotoxin?

No. Large numbers on packages may refer to total honey, extract, blend or another marketing measure. Without a clearly identified analyte, unit and validated test result, the number does not establish toxin content or a safe serving size.

Is it safe because it is natural or imported from Nepal or Turkey?

No. Plant-derived toxins can cause severe poisoning. Geographic origin, “organic,” “lab tested,” and “medicinal” claims do not establish safety. Grayanotoxin concentrations may vary widely between samples.

Does mad honey have proven medical benefits?

Mad honey has a history of folk use for several conditions, but reliable clinical evidence for therapeutic benefit is limited. The risk of bradycardia and hypotension makes self-treatment especially concerning.

What if someone has taken mad honey?

If the person collapses, has chest pain, faints, has trouble breathing or is difficult to wake, call 911 immediately. For other suspected exposures in the United States, call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222. Do not induce vomiting. Keep the package for clinicians.

What is the legal status?

There is no single rule that makes all products marketed as “mad honey” lawful or unlawful. Requirements depend on ingredients, intended use, labeling and jurisdiction. Retail availability is not proof that a product has been approved for safety or efficacy.

What parents and communities should know

Warning signs of poisoning

  • Dizziness, weakness, nausea or vomiting after eating a honey product
  • Fainting, severe lightheadedness or confusion
  • Unusually slow heartbeat or low blood pressure
  • Chest pain, irregular heartbeat or difficulty staying awake
  • Unclear labeling, extravagant milligram claims or no ingredient-specific test report

What to do

  • Call 911 for collapse, fainting, chest pain, breathing difficulty or severe symptoms.
  • Call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 for suspected exposures.
  • Save the packaging, ingredient list and any batch or lot number.
  • Report adverse events to FDA MedWatch.
  • Use the site's Report a Product page to document local retail concerns.
