Opioids

Kratom, 7-OH, tianeptine and emerging opioid-active compounds. Recognize the names, understand the evidence, and know what to do in an emergency.

See the SubstancesProtect Your Family

An opioid-active product does not need to look like a prescription drug.

Shots, tablets, capsules and powders may be marketed with botanical imagery or unfamiliar chemical names. Some contain kratom alkaloids or concentrated derivatives; others contain unrelated drugs such as tianeptine. Their risks, evidence and legal status are not identical. A product's label alone cannot establish its contents.

Concentrated commercial products are documented. A naturally occurring trace alkaloid is not the same exposure as a high-dose retail formulation.

Mitragynine pseudoindoxyl requires compound-specific identification; packaging may not disclose it.

Evidence

Laboratory opioid activity does not prove human safety, clinical potency or commercial availability.

What parents should look for

Product and packaging clues

  • Names such as 7-OH, 7-hydroxy, pseudoindoxyl, MP, mitragynine or tianeptine
  • Small shots, chewable tablets, gummies, capsules and concentrated powders
  • Claims such as “extra strength,” “research use only” or “not for human consumption”
  • Ingredient lists without clear amounts or third-party analytical results
  • Brand names and formulas that change faster than laws and testing programs

Possible signs of opioid toxicity

  • Unusual sleepiness or inability to wake the person
  • Slow, irregular or absent breathing
  • Gurgling, choking or snoring sounds with unresponsiveness
  • Blue or gray lips, especially with impaired breathing
  • Pinpoint pupils can occur, but their absence does not rule out overdose

Recognize an emergency and take action

If someone is unresponsive or breathing abnormally, call 911 immediately. Give naloxone if available and follow the device instructions; additional doses may be needed. Start CPR or rescue breathing as directed by the dispatcher, and stay with the person. Naloxone may reverse opioid effects but will not reverse every ingredient in a mixed product.

After a suspected exposure

  • For poisoning advice in the U.S., call Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222.
  • Keep the packaging or photograph the ingredients for clinicians.
  • Do not make the person vomit or leave an unconscious person alone.
  • Seek clinical help for dependence or withdrawal; stopping suddenly may require medical support.

Report and document

  • Record the product name, lot number, purchase location and date.
  • Report suspected adverse events through FDA MedWatch.
  • Use the site's existing reporting and parent resources.
  • Distinguish verified laboratory findings from advertising claims.
